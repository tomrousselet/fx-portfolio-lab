\paragraph{Quotation convention.}
FRED quotes the euro, the pound and the Australian and New Zealand dollars in US dollars per unit of foreign currency, and the other currencies in foreign currency per US dollar.
All spot rates are converted to US dollars per unit of foreign currency, so that an increase of $S_t$ is always an appreciation of the foreign currency, consistently with equation~\eqref{eq:rx-deposit}.

\paragraph{Sample.}
The sample starts in January 1999, with the introduction of the euro.
A currency enters the panel as soon as its spot rate and interest rate are both available (Decision~D9): the Swiss franc in August 1999 and the yen in May 2002, all other currencies in February 1999.
The euro and sterling rates published by the OECD stop in January 2026, so these two currencies leave the panel after February 2026.
A balanced panel (May 2002 -- February 2026) is kept as a robustness check.

\subsection{Descriptive statistics}

Table~\ref{tab:rx} reports the monthly excess returns of a long position in each currency against the US dollar, computed as described in Section~\ref{sec:rx}.
The row EW is the equally weighted portfolio of the available currencies, rebalanced monthly; its return is the cross-sectional average of the excess returns.
In the literature it is called the \emph{dollar factor}, since it is short the US dollar against all other currencies \citep{lustig2011}.

Three facts emerge from Table~\ref{tab:rx} and Figure~\ref{fig:cumrx}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Volatility.} Annualised volatilities lie between 8\% and 13\%, below those of an equity index (15--20\%) but far from negligible: a currency position is a risky asset in its own right. The EW portfolio has a volatility of 8.1\%, below that of every currency, as predicted by diversification.
  \item \textbf{Carry and returns line up.} The currencies with the highest carry (AUD, NZD) have the highest average excess returns, and the yen, the currency with the lowest carry, has the lowest. This is the pattern exploited by the carry trade (Section~\ref{sec:portfolios}). The Swiss franc is an exception: its carry is negative, yet its excess return is positive, because it appreciated against the dollar; this is consistent with its status as a safe-haven currency. Figure~\ref{fig:cumrx} also shows the asymmetry of carry returns: the cumulative value of the Australian dollar position fell by 32\% between June 2008 and January 2009, more than ten years of its average excess return (2.5\% a year) lost in seven months, which is the crash risk documented by \citet{brunnermeier2008}.
  \item \textbf{Average returns are imprecise.} The $t$-statistic of a mean return is approximately the annualised Sharpe ratio times the square root of the number of years. With Sharpe ratios below 0.25 and about 27 years of data, no individual mean is significantly different from zero at the 5\% level ($0.22\times\sqrt{27.5}\approx 1.15 < 1.96$). Expected returns are therefore much harder to estimate than volatilities, which is the practical argument for the minimum-variance portfolio \citep{milhau2026}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Forward rates} are not freely available; they are implied by CIP (equation~\ref{eq:rx-forward}). Since 2008, CIP deviations of the order of a few tens of basis points per year exist \citep{du2018}.
  \item \textbf{Interest rates} are monthly averages of interbank rates, not the rate observed on the day a position is opened.
  \item \textbf{Maturity mismatch}: 3-month rates are used to price 1-month forwards, because 1-month rates are not freely available for all currencies. The error on the carry is of the order of the slope of the money-market curve between 1 and 3 months.
  \item \textbf{Euro and sterling rates} stop in January 2026 in the OECD data; they could be extended with the ECB and Bank of England series.
  \item \textbf{Transaction costs}: bid--ask spreads are not in the data and must be modelled \todo{reference and figures}.
\end{itemize}
