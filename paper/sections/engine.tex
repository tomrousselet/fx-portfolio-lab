\section{Valuation and backtesting engine}\label{sec:engine}

\subsection{Portfolio valuation}\label{sec:valuation}

The engine values a portfolio by propagating the dollar amounts held in each constituent, following the general method of \citet{milhau2026}: amounts are updated with equation~\eqref{eq:amount-update} between rebalancing dates and reset by the allocation rule on rebalancing dates.
Working with amounts rather than weights has two advantages.
First, it handles any rebalancing frequency with the same code (daily rebalancing and buy-and-hold are the two limiting cases).
Second, it gives the amount traded at each date directly, from which transaction costs are computed (Decision~D4).

\paragraph{Verification.}
The engine reproduces the worked examples of the course: the valuation of an equally weighted portfolio of five US stocks between 2 and 5 January 2015 (final value \$978.21, \$6.37 traded to return to equal weights), the drift of weights in a buy-and-hold portfolio, and the compounding of returns.
These examples are encoded as automated tests (Appendix~\ref{app:software}).

\subsection{Monthly currency excess returns}\label{sec:rx}

\paragraph{Sampling.}
The month-end spot rate is the last available daily rate of the month (Decision~D8); if the data stop before the last business day of the final month, that month is dropped, so that no mid-month rate is treated as a month-end rate.
The OECD interest rate of month $t$ is an average over the month, so it is dated at the end of month $t$, when it is fully known.

\paragraph{Forward rate and excess return.}
At the end of month $t$, the one-month forward rate implied by CIP is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwd-monthly}
  F_t = S_t \, \frac{1 + i_t/12}{1 + i^*_t/12},
\end{equation}
and the excess return of a long forward position held over month $t+1$ is (Decision~D11)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rx-monthly}
  rx_{t+1} = \frac{S_{t+1}}{F_t} - 1.
\end{equation}
The \emph{carry} of the position, known at $t$, is $S_t/F_t - 1 \approx (i_t^* - i_t)/12$: the return earned if the spot rate does not move.

\paragraph{Timing.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rx-monthly} uses only $S_t$, $i_t$ and $i^*_t$, observed at the end of month $t$, to open the position; the outcome $S_{t+1}$ is observed one month later.
A test checks that changing the interest rates at $t+1$ or the spot rates after $t+1$ leaves $rx_{t+1}$ unchanged.

\subsection{Backtesting portfolios of forwards}\label{sec:fwd-backtest}

The valuation of Section~\ref{sec:valuation} assumes a fully invested portfolio, whose weights sum to one.
A currency strategy is different: a forward requires no capital, so the capital $V_t$ is held as collateral, and the strategy is described by \emph{notional weights} $w_{t,i} = N_{t,i}/V_t$, the notional of the forward on currency $i$ divided by the capital.
These weights need not sum to one; a long-short strategy has $\sum_i w_{t,i} = 0$.

The engine applies the amount-propagation method to notionals (Decision~D4).
At the end of month $t$, the strategy decides target weights $w_t$ from information available at $t$, and the notionals are set to $N_{t,i} = w_{t,i} V_t$.
Moving from the notionals of the previous month to $N_t$ costs $C_t$ (Section~\ref{sec:costs}), paid immediately.
Over month $t+1$ the forwards earn $\sum_i N_{t,i}\, rx_{t+1,i}$ and the collateral may earn the US rate, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwd-value}
  V_{t+1} = V_t - C_t + \sum_{i=1}^N N_{t,i}\, rx_{t+1,i} + V_t\,\frac{i_t}{12},
  \qquad
  \frac{V_{t+1}}{V_t} - 1 = \w_t'\vect{rx}_{t+1} - \frac{C_t}{V_t} + \frac{i_t}{12}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the course's fundamental property $r_p = \w'\vect{r}$ applied to excess returns; the last term is omitted when excess returns are reported, which is the default in this paper.
A position in a currency whose excess return is not available is rejected by the engine, so that a strategy cannot silently trade a currency that has left the panel.

\subsection{Transaction costs}\label{sec:costs}

\paragraph{Model (Decision D14).}
A forward position kept from one month to the next is rolled with an FX swap, which simultaneously closes the maturing forward and opens a new one; a swap is cheaper than two outright trades.
Any change of position is an outright trade.
For each currency, with $N^{-}$ the previous notional and $N$ the new one, the cost is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
  C_i = c^{\mathrm{swap}}_i \times \underbrace{\min(|N^-|, |N|)\,\mathbb{1}\{N^- N > 0\}}_{\text{notional rolled}}
      + c^{\mathrm{out}}_i \times \underbrace{|N - N^-|}_{\text{notional traded}},
\end{equation}
where $c^{\mathrm{swap}}_i$ and $c^{\mathrm{out}}_i$ are half-spreads in fraction of notional.
A change of sign keeps nothing: the position is closed and reopened.
A model that charged only the notional traded would ignore the roll and understate the cost of strategies that hold their positions, such as the carry trade.

\paragraph{Level (Decision D15).}
Bid--ask spreads are not freely available.
The base case is therefore an \emph{assumption} by liquidity tier, not an estimate: outright and swap half-spreads of 2 and 1 basis points for the most liquid currencies (EUR, JPY, GBP, CHF, CAD), 3 and 1.5 for AUD and NZD, and 5 and 2.5 for NOK and SEK.
Rather than depending on these figures, every strategy is reported for the base case scaled by 0, 1, 2 and 5, together with its \emph{break-even cost scale}: the multiple of the base case at which its average net return falls to zero.
Readers can compare this multiple with their own trading costs, which differ widely between an interbank dealer and a corporate client.

\subsection{Performance and risk measures}\label{sec:metrics}

For monthly returns $r_t$ with sample mean $\bar r$ and standard deviation $s$ over $n$ months, the paper reports the annualised mean $12\bar r$, the annualised volatility $\sqrt{12}\,s$, the Sharpe ratio $\sqrt{12}\,\bar r/s$ (the returns being already in excess of the US rate), the $t$-statistic of the mean $\bar r/(s/\sqrt n)$, which equals the Sharpe ratio times the square root of the number of years, the skewness and excess kurtosis of monthly returns, the maximum drawdown, the annual cost $12\,\overline{C/V}$ and the annual turnover $12\,\overline{\sum_i|N_t - N^-_{t}|/V}$.
Skewness is the key measure of crash risk: a strategy that earns small regular gains and suffers rare large losses has a negative skewness \citep{brunnermeier2008}.

Three points are worth noting.
First, with zero costs the engine reproduces the mean of the cross-sectional average of Table~\ref{tab:rx} (0.37\% a year), an independent check of the backtest.
Second, even a strategy that almost never trades pays about 0.18\% a year in the base case, because its whole notional is rolled every month; the outright turnover is only 0.34 times the capital a year, coming from the monthly resizing of positions and from currencies entering the panel.
This confirms that ignoring the roll (alternative of Decision~D14) would understate costs.
Third, the break-even cost scale is \input{tables/dollar_factor_breakeven}\unskip~times the base case: with costs about twice the base case, the small premium of the dollar factor disappears.
The dollar factor is not a strategy in itself but the benchmark against which the long-short strategies of Section~\ref{sec:portfolios} are compared; since those are long and short at the same time, they do not earn this premium and must be justified by their own returns.
